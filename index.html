01 / The idea

More than media literacy. Media for a purpose.

Campus Voices Lab invites students to identify issues or promising initiatives on their campus, listen to the people affected, verify information, and create media that helps others understand or respond.

PROGRAM PURPOSE

From passive audiences to active contributors.

Help university students become critical investigators, ethical storytellers, and contributors to their campus community through source evaluation, interviews, field documentation, collaborative media production, and evidence-informed recommendations.

THE APPROACH

Learn by investigating and making.

Small teams select a campus cause, develop an inquiry, gather perspectives and evidence, create weekly artifacts, and publish a final Campus Media Patch—a story plus a realistic action brief. Problems do not need to be solved in eight weeks to be investigated responsibly.

Investigate critically

Ask good questions, compare sources, represent uncertainty, and check competing claims.

Create ethically

Seek permission, interview respectfully, credit sources, and produce accessible media.

Contribute constructively

Inform peers, identify existing supports, and propose feasible next steps.

02 / Program sequence

Eight weeks. One connected story.

Select any week to explore the activities and deliverable. Every artifact feeds the next, so the final story is evidence-based rather than an isolated production assignment.

05 / Embedded research

The making process is also the research context.

This small exploratory study would examine how students investigate campus questions, evaluate competing evidence, work collaboratively, and experience agency through participatory media production.

PROPOSED STUDY

From Campus Issues to Media Action

Exploring critical media literacy, student agency, and participatory media production in a university library-based learning lab.

Design: An exploratory qualitative case study, with two student teams as embedded cases and analysis of artifacts over eight weeks.

GUIDING QUESTIONS

What do we want to understand?

  • How do teams define issues, compare perspectives, and verify evidence?
  • How do inquiry and source practices change over the eight weeks?
  • How do participants understand their ability to inform and influence campus conversations?
POTENTIAL DATA SOURCES

Trace the learning journey.

  • Pre/post source-evaluation tasks
  • Campus Issue Briefs and observation journals
  • Interview plans, fieldnotes, and Evidence Receipts
  • Draft scripts, production logs, and final media
  • Reflection interviews and showcase feedback
RESEARCH OUTPUTS

Useful beyond the pilot.

  • A small exploratory manuscript or conference presentation
  • A refined eight-week media inquiry curriculum
  • A practical rubric for evidence-based media work
  • Feasibility evidence for a larger youth/community version
Ethics and scope: The pilot and the research study should have separate, voluntary consent processes. Obtain relevant UArk IRB guidance before collecting research data. Secure interview and recording permissions, avoid identifying non-consenting people in public outputs, and use approved storage. With 6–8 participants, findings would be exploratory, not causal or broadly generalizable.
06 / Pilot at a glance

The final deliverable: a Campus Media Patch.

Each team produces a 4–7 minute documentary, podcast, or multimedia story; a one-page action brief; a transparent Evidence Receipt; and a short campus showcase presentation. What matters is an informed, ethical, actionable contribution—not a promise to solve a complex issue in eight weeks.
